Googlebook is Google's new laptop line, built on the Android stack with ChromeOS desktop foundations. Because it runs Android, apps don't need a separate build — **Wallpaper Reactor is the same Android app you'd put on a phone**, and live wallpapers work on it: the wallpaper animates behind your windows and on the lock screen.

Here is exactly what we've done. **We ran Google's own Googlebook OS image (the Dell Googlebook's ARM64 recovery image, Android 17) in a virtual machine on an Apple Silicon Mac**, using the open-source gbos-vm project. Every screenshot below comes from that VM. **We have not run it on physical Googlebook hardware**, and we haven't installed it from Google Play on a real Googlebook. Wallpaper Reactor isn't certified by Google for Googlebook and doesn't carry a Google Play desktop badge.

## Install it

1. Open **Google Play** on your Googlebook.
2. Search for **Wallpaper Reactor**, or go straight to the [Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=app.wallpaperreactor).
3. Install, then open it.

It's free, and browsing, downloading and setting wallpapers need no account. Our VM can't sign in to Google, so the Play step itself is the part we haven't seen on a Googlebook. The Play listing has also been hidden from keyboard-and-mouse PCs because it required a touchscreen; **the next update drops that requirement**. Until then, or if you'd rather not use Play, the Android APK is on the [releases page](/releases/). We installed the APK in the VM.

## Set a wallpaper

It's the same flow as on any Android device, and the [Android walkthrough](/blog/how-to-set-live-wallpaper-android/) has screenshots of each step.

1. Open **Browse** and pick a wallpaper. **Search**, **Sort** and the **Type**, **Colors** and **Tags** filters narrow the list.
2. Open it and choose **Download**. The files stay on your device.
3. Choose **Set wallpaper**.
4. Android opens its own live wallpaper screen for Wallpaper Reactor. **Confirm it there** — Android only runs a live wallpaper that you confirm yourself. It asks whether it's for the home screen, the lock screen or both.

### What we haven't confirmed

On Android 16 (QPR3) and newer, and on Android 17, **Android blocks third-party live wallpapers on a connected external display** — a monitor, or desktop mode. That's an Android platform policy, and we explain it in [Do live wallpapers work on Android Desktop Mode?](/blog/android-desktop-mode-live-wallpapers/).

A Googlebook is a laptop with its own screen, and that policy didn't apply to it in our VM: the wallpaper showed behind windows and on the lock screen. What we haven't done is run any of this on physical Googlebook hardware, or install from Google Play on one. If you try it on a Googlebook, we'd like to hear what you see.

## What it can run

Wallpaper Reactor isn't just video loops. On Googlebook OS in the VM, every wallpaper type rendered as the live wallpaper:

- **Image**, including animated WebP and GIF.
- **Video**: MP4 and WebM.
- **Shaders**: GLSL, drawn live.
- **Rive animations**: vector animations, drawn from shapes rather than stored as pixels.
- **Scenes** and **interactive 3D** built in Godot.
- **Websites**: HTML pages as wallpapers.
- **Compositions**.

Some of the fixes that made this work land in **the next update**: the video and Rive fallbacks, catalogue shader conversion and composition routing. Until it ships, a few wallpaper types may not draw on Googlebook OS yet.

As of 0.30.0 you can upload scenes, interactive 3D (Godot) and website wallpapers. You can also use your own file without an account through **Quick Wallpaper** on the **Create** tab: pick a file and it's applied. There's more on that in [setting any video as a live wallpaper](/blog/set-any-video-as-live-wallpaper/).

Interactive wallpapers (Scenes and Godot) respond to touch and mouse input. **Touch Interaction** in **Settings → Wallpaper Settings** turns that off.

## Using the app in a desktop window

Wallpaper Reactor on Googlebook OS runs in a window you can resize and maximize, and the layout follows the window. Wide, it shows the library with a details panel beside it; narrow, it switches to a bottom tab bar.

In the VM:

- **Resizing and maximizing** worked. The layout reflows live as the window changes size.
- **Mouse**: clicks, hover, wheel scrolling, right-clicking a card for a menu, and typing in fields all work.
- **Keyboard**: Tab and the arrow keys move focus, with a visible focus ring.
- **Gamepad**: a controller moves focus and steps through Browse, details and back.
- **Drag and drop**: in 0.30.0 you can drop a file on the window in desktop mode, and files you open with or share to Wallpaper Reactor are picked up directly.
- **Full screen**: F11 toggles fullscreen on Android 15 and later.

There are keyboard shortcuts for the whole app too. On Android and ChromeOS, the modifier is Ctrl:

| Keys | Does |
|---|---|
| Ctrl+F, `/` | Focus the Browse search field |
| Ctrl+1 … Ctrl+9 | Switch between the tabs, in order |
| Ctrl+, | Settings |
| Alt+Left, Ctrl+[ | Back |
| F11 | Full screen |
| `?` | Show the shortcuts sheet |

Press **?** in the app for the full list.

The VM has no battery, no real touchscreen and a virtual GPU, so frame rates and power behaviour on a real Googlebook may differ.

## Battery and performance

A laptop is a battery you carry around, so this matters more here than on a desktop PC. **Performance modes are free for everyone as of 0.30.0.** They set the frame rate cap and the resolution a wallpaper renders at, under **Settings → Wallpaper Settings**:

| Mode | Frame rate | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Battery Saver | 15 fps | 50% |
| Balanced (default) | 30 fps | 75% |
| High FPS | Uncapped | 75% |
| High Resolution | 30 fps | 100% |
| High Quality | 60 fps | 90% |
| Max Quality | Uncapped | 100% |

Battery Saver asks for far less work than Max Quality, so it's the one to reach for when you're away from a charger. Changes take effect on the running wallpaper.

**Pause on Low Power Mode** is on by default: the wallpaper pauses while the system's low power or battery saver mode is on. We haven't checked how that interacts with Googlebook's own power settings.
